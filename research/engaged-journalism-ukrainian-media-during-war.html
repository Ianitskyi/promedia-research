Changes in Ukrainian News Media Activities During the War: Theoretical Aspect

Abstract. Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 has affected all spheres of life, including the media. New security-related and administrative constraints have been added to the global technological, economic and political challenges. Ukrainian media had already been weakened by the 2014 Russian aggression and the COVID-19 pandemic. Under such conditions, many outlets turned to engaged journalism, notably membership-based revenue models. Following the 2022 invasion, the situation further deteriorated. During the first 1,000 days, 329 Ukrainian outlets were closed. In spite of this, media consumption research indicates that the audience's trust in Ukrainian media increased after the invasion. One possible driver of resilience is the development of communities around media organisations, such as engaged journalism.

The aim of this article is to systematise theoretical foundations and approaches to engaged journalism, situating Ukrainian media within this aspect and providing a robust theoretical base for subsequent empirical studies. This article is a theory-driven review supplemented by illustrative empirical examples. Methodologically, this study employs analysis, synthesis, formalisation, generalisation and comparison. The main output is a seven-component conceptual model of a media community as the most active audience's segment, enabling comparison of media communities not only by quantitative but also by qualitative characteristics.

Keywords: engaged journalism, participatory journalism, media consumption, trust in media, information resilience, media landscape transformation.

Relevance of the problem

Russia's full-scale invasion on February 24, 2022 placed a double burden on Ukrainian newsrooms: on top of the global challenges journalism faces everywhere, war added a set of challenges unique to Ukraine. Among the country-specific ones, the author highlights a sharp deterioration in the security situation (329 outlets closed during the first 1,000 days of the full-scale war, per the Institute of Mass Information), the forced merger of leading TV channels into a single "Yedyni Novyny" (United News) broadcast under a March 18, 2022 decision of the National Security and Defense Council, and the mass displacement of newsrooms and journalists from occupied territories.

The author groups global challenges into technological, political, and economic ones. Technological challenges blur the line between professional and non-professional journalism and raise new ethical questions — including whether detachment is even possible for a journalist covering a war for their own country's independence. Political challenges stem from a worldwide crisis of trust in traditional media as information "gatekeepers," amid rising populism and politicians' (including Volodymyr Zelensky's, since 2019) preference for direct communication with audiences via social media over classic media. Economic challenges are the cumulative effect of the above: a shrinking ad market, the winding-down of donor funding (including USAID), and the absence of systemic state support, compounded by a staffing crisis driven by mobilization, migration, and journalists leaving the profession.

Problem statement

Against this backdrop, media-consumption research in Ukraine (Internews Ukraine, 2025) records a paradox: despite all the difficulties, audience trust in Ukrainian media rose after the invasion. The author stresses that the causes of this rise are only now being clarified, and names the spread of engaged journalism — specifically, the launch of reader communities with membership-based revenue models — as one hypothesis. This remains a hypothesis rather than a proven conclusion: the author explicitly notes that confirming the link would require a separate empirical study.

The article documents a shift in scale: while in 2021 paid reader communities were isolated experiments by individual newsrooms, by 2025 community-building had become a trend — the Institute for War and Peace Reporting ran training for more than 30 regional media outlets that already have, or are building, communities (the author is personally involved in organizing this training). According to data cited by representatives of The Ukrainians Media and The Kyiv Independent in October 2025, the most successful "community-first" outlets generate more than half their budget from voluntary reader support.

The author delineates the boundaries of "engaged journalism": a systematic form of interaction between media and audience in which the most active segment of the audience takes part in the newsroom's work. Participatory journalism — audience involvement in content creation — is presented as just one manifestation of this broader phenomenon. The least intensive form of engagement is described as reflective interaction (comments, letters, donations); the most intensive is co-governance, where the audience influences a media outlet's strategic and personnel decisions. The article is explicit that a paid-access (paywall) model is not, by itself, engaged journalism — though paywalled media can also practice engaged journalism for other reasons.

State of research

The theoretical framework opens with Jürgen Habermas's public sphere — a space of rational-critical discussion that legitimizes power. The author traces how technological change (the shift from vertical to horizontal networked communication, per Manuel Castells) blurred the line between media and audience, creating both risks of information fragmentation via social-media algorithms (Cass Sunstein) and potential for participatory culture (Henry Jenkins).

The article then draws together several traditions of community research more broadly: Carole Pateman's theory of participation and collective decision-making, Sherry Arnstein's "ladder of citizen participation" and Nico Carpentier's levels of media participation, the classic uses-and-gratifications theory of Elihu Katz, Jay Blumler, and Michael Gurevitch (which the author narrows to two need types — practical-cognitive and value-emotional), Robert Putnam's social capital theory, Zizi Papacharissi's concept of affective publics (instant mobilization through shared emotional resonance rather than argument), and Etienne Wenger's communities-of-practice theory (a community's resilience is built through regular interaction, shared rituals, and norms). Elinor Ostrom's economic theory of commons governance is applied separately — information within a community is treated as a shared resource that can be managed as sustainably as a material one.

At the intersection of these theories, the author also reframes the journalist's role: the classic "gatekeeping" model of David Manning White gives way to one in which the journalist acts more as a curator and conversation moderator, co-creating meaning with the audience (drawing on Alfred Hermida, Chris Peters, and Marcel Broersma), while retaining the capacity to set the agenda (Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw) and frame issues (Robert Entman).

The theoretical account is illustrated with three Ukrainian examples. The Kyiv Independent's community numbers over 22,000 members (as of October 2025), and reader remittances form over 70% of the newsroom's budget — the author reads it as an affective community with clear formalization. The Ukrainians Media unites over 3,500 people and is treated as an attempt to build deep social capital with a nationally active audience. Ukraїner's volunteer model is cited as a unique example of combining content co-creation with building a community of responsible citizens.

Unresolved issues and aim

The author frames the core problem as follows: existing descriptions of media communities, while grounded in theory, give no methodological basis for comparing communities across newsrooms. The article's stated aim is therefore to develop an original conceptual model for analysing and comparing media communities, suitable for further empirical research.

Conceptual model: seven parameters for describing a media community

The article's main output is the author's seven-component model, which allows any media community to be described not only quantitatively (number of members, contribution size) but also qualitatively. Below are the model's parameters with a short description and theoretical grounding for each, following the article's original table.

Seven-component model for analysing media communities (Ianitskyi, 2026)
ParameterWhat it describesTheoretical grounding
1. Value propositionWhy the community exists; the shared idea that unites membersThe concept of "domain" in Etienne Wenger's communities of practice
2. Level of formalizationClear rules, procedures, division of roles (e.g., a community manager)Elinor Ostrom's design principles for robust institutions
3. Member contributionWhat resources members invest — money, time, content, expertiseHenry Jenkins's participatory culture
4. Level of interactionHow engaged members are: from reflective interaction and consultation to co-creation and co-governanceSherry Arnstein's "ladder of participation"; Nico Carpentier's models of participation
5. Shared practicesRecurring rituals, events, symbols, and traditions of the communityEtienne Wenger's communities of practice
6. Value-emotional factorsAffective reasons for engagement — belonging, solidarityZizi Papacharissi's "affective publics"; uses and gratifications theory
7. Practical-cognitive benefitsRational benefits of membership — knowledge, connections, exclusive information, discountsUses and gratifications theory (Katz, Blumler, Gurevitch)

By the author's own account, this matrix lets researchers compare different media communities, identify strengths and weaknesses in how they are organized, and assess communities' potential both for a newsroom's financial sustainability and for strengthening democratic practice more broadly.

Conclusions

The author concludes that the spread of engaged journalism in Ukraine during the war is not a situational reaction to economic and security pressures, but a logical consequence of global shifts in the media landscape that simply manifest with particular intensity in Ukraine's wartime context. On this reading, media communities become platforms for sustaining public discussion and strengthening civic solidarity precisely when trust in traditional institutions is unstable and the information space is fragmented.

At the same time, the article explicitly warns against overgeneralizing: the rising attention to engaged journalism is occurring amid wartime emotional mobilization — patriotism, volunteering, civic solidarity. Conclusions about the success and sustainability of these models therefore cannot be automatically transferred to peacetime contexts or other countries; this is set out as a direction for further empirical research, including on post-war material.
